> Judge: The warm bedtime wind-down and the gentle, child-sized mystery of the missing bell make this story easy and cozy to read aloud. Its most important fix is the narrated line about mixed feelings, which states the lesson instead of letting the actions show it.

Alice lived in a yellow house at the end of Thistle Lane, and her best friend Bob was a cat. He was a big, fluffy, marmalade cat with white socks and a silver bell on a red ribbon around his neck.

Every evening, Bob and Alice followed the same bedtime plan. First bath, then pajamas, then one story, then Bob curled up on the end of her bed like a warm, purring loaf of bread.

But on this particular evening, something was wrong. Alice waited for the jingle when Bob padded up the stairs. Tink. Tink. Nothing.

"Bob," said Alice, "where is your bell?"

Bob looked down at his neck. He blinked his green eyes and gave a small, surprised "mrrp?" as if the bell had wandered off all by itself.

Alice checked under the rug. She checked behind the bookshelf. She even checked the bathtub, which was silly, because Bob hated water and would never go near it.

"Tink," said Bob hopefully, giving his head a shake.

Nothing.

Mum came to the bottom of the stairs with a basket of warm towels. "Lost something, sweetheart?"

"Bob's bell," said Alice. "It's gone."

"Gone?" Mum said, looking very serious, as if a cat without a bell were the strangest thing in the world. "Well, I expect you'll find it. Bells are rather good at being found, if you listen carefully."

Alice sat on the top stair and thought hard. Listen carefully. But how could you listen for something that wasn't making a sound?

Bob came and sat beside her. He pressed his warm side against her arm. He smelled of sunshine and dusty garden and, just a little, of fish from supper.

"Let's be quiet," Alice whispered. "Really quiet. Then we'll listen."

So they sat. The clock in the hall went tock. Outside, an owl said hoo. The fridge hummed its low, sleepy hum. Bob's whiskers twitched.

Then, very faintly, from somewhere near the front door: tink.

Bob's ears shot up. Alice jumped to her feet. They tiptoed down the stairs one step at a time, Bob's tail held high like a question mark.

Tink. Tink-a-tink.

The sound was coming from the boots. Alice's red wellingtons stood in a row beside the door, still muddy from the afternoon in the garden. She peered into the left boot. Nothing. She peered into the right boot, and something small and shiny winked back at her.

She pulled it out, and there, dangling from her fingers on its red ribbon, was Bob's silver bell.

"Bob!" Alice gasped. "You batted it into my boot!"

Bob looked at the bell. He looked at Alice. Then he stared very hard at the ceiling, as if the ceiling had done it.

"Mrrp," said Bob, which is cat for oops.

Alice laughed, but she also felt a tiny bit cross, because she had been wearing those boots all afternoon with a bell in one of them and never noticed. Feeling cross and laughing at the same time is a strange, wobbly feeling, Alice thought, but it is allowed.

She tied the bell back on his neck. Tink, went the bell, happy again.

"Next time," said Alice, "you tell me before you play hide-and-seek with it."

Bob rubbed his head against her knee. That was cat for I'm sorry, and thank you for finding it.

Upstairs, they climbed into bed. Mum switched off the big light and left the little lamp glowing, the color of warm honey. Alice read one page of their story about a sleepy dragon. Then she read another page, slower.

Bob curled up at her feet, one white sock tucked under his chin. Each breath lifted his fur and set it down again, soft as a pillow.

Tink, went the bell. Tink. Tink...

Alice's eyelids felt heavy. The owl outside had stopped calling. The clock in the hall went tock, tock, quieter now, quieter still.

"Goodnight, Bob," she whispered.

Bob's purr rumbled, warm and slow, like a tiny engine that knew the way home.

'It looks tired,' said Bob. 'Let's take it home.'

Silver light spilled back over the fence. The pond sparkled. The lantern bumped softly against Alice's boot, as if to say thank you.

Alice held out the flashlight to Bob. 'Your turn to lead,' she said.

Bob sat up tall and clicked it on with his paw. A little beam wobbled across the grass, and he padded back toward the house with his tail held high. Alice followed, yawning, with Bob sneezing once more. Alice climbed into bed, and Bob curled up on her feet. Outside, the pond whispered. The apple leaves rustled, slow and sleepy. The moon hung steady over the garden, and the flashlight clicked off.

'Goodnight, Bob,' Alice whispered.

'Goodnight, Alice,' Bob whispered back, and the garden grew quiet.
