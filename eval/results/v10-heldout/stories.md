## penguin-snow (normal)

**Request:** A penguin who has never seen snow falling

**Status:** ok (expected ok) · language: en

### Writer only (no judge): *Pip and the Shy Snowflake*
0 issues · mean 4.43 · passed 0% of reviews · 345 words (target 600) · grade 1.6


> Judge: The story's biggest strength is its gentle refrain and the tender moment where Pip learns to be still so the snowflake can land. It would be improved by a little more variety in the middle, such as one more silly moment from Otto, to lift the playful energy before the calm close.

Pip was a penguin who lived by the cold blue sea. She had never seen snow.

One morning the sky turned grey, and the air went very quiet. Pip waddled to the edge of the ice. Something white was drifting down from above.

"Flakes!" squeaked her friend Otto, a fat little seal with a curly whisker. "Catch one on your beak!"

Pip stretched her neck. She snapped her beak. Nothing. The flake spun past her and landed on her toe. It was gone in a blink.

"Oh," said Pip. "It tickled."

Otto flapped his flippers. "Try again! Try again!"

Pip hopped left. She hopped right. She spun in a circle, and her tail feathers went wobble, wobble, wobble. The snow kept falling, soft and slow, and it never stayed still for her.

"Snow is shy," giggled Otto. "It only likes to be caught by someone who is still."

Pip looked up. A flake drifted down, down, down, right in front of her nose. She did not snap. She did not hop. She just stood very, very still.

The flake settled on the tip of her beak. It was cold. It was cool. It was as light as a whisper.

"Oh!" said Pip, and her eyes went big and round. "It is like a tiny star that fell down to visit me."

Otto clapped his flippers. Plop, plop, plop! Pip laughed, and the flake melted into a single drop of water.

All afternoon, the snow came down. It covered the rocks in white. It covered the ice in soft, fluffy blankets. Pip made a snow-puffin shape by flapping her wings on the ground. Otto made a snow-seal, with a lumpy nose. They sat side by side and watched the flakes dance.

The sky began to go dim. The snow slowed to a sleepy drift. Pip tucked her feet under her warm feathers.

"Goodnight, snow," she whispered.

Otto yawned. "Goodnight, Pip."

And the snow kept falling, soft and slow, on the ice and the rocks and the two friends, until everything was quiet, and white, and still.

## blanket-fort (normal)

The blanket fort had been Noor's idea, but it was the twins who built it, and it was the twins who argued about it all afternoon. Noor was eight, and her brother Tansy was eight too, because they were twins, and that meant they agreed on almost nothing.

"The roof goes here," said Noor, dragging the big green quilt across the sofa.

"The roof goes there," said Tansy, pulling the quilt back. "Otherwise the torches won't fit."

Noor sighed. Tansy was right about the torches. She had three of them, and she loved them more than almost anything. She clicked one on and a warm yellow circle wobbled across the rug, like a little sun that had lost its way.

By the time the fort was finished, it sagged in the middle and smelled faintly of toast. Still, it was theirs. They crawled inside with two cushions, a bowl of crackers, and a notebook full of secret codes.

"Tonight we test the code," Noor whispered. "If I tap three times on the wall, you tap back two."

*Tap, tap, tap.*

Nothing happened.

*Tap, tap.*

From the kitchen came a loud, squeaky sound, like a shoe on a wet floor: *SKREEEK.* Then a clatter. Then a small, grumpy voice said, "Who put the cat food in the fridge?"

Tansy snorted so hard that a cracker flew out of his mouth. Noor giggled until she hiccupped, and the hiccup made her giggle more, and soon the whole fort shook like a jelly on a plate.

When they finally stopped, they had a problem. Their dad was coming to check on them in five minutes, and the fort had no way to be seen from the hallway. Noor's codes were perfect, but nobody could read them from outside.

"We need a signal," said Tansy. "A torch signal. Only the right flashes would mean we're safe."

Noor grinned. She had an idea, and she tapped it out in her notebook: a long flash, two short flashes, then one long flash. The torch went on and off, on and off. A pattern of gold danced across the ceiling.

They practised until their thumbs ached. Long, short, short, long. Long, long, short. Each time they got it right, Tansy cheered, and each time they got it wrong, Noor groaned and blamed the torch.

Just before the five minutes were up, Dad's footsteps came padding down the hall. Noor clicked the torch, long, short, short, long. Then she held her breath.

The door creaked open. Dad peered into the living room. He looked at the sagging quilt, the cracker crumbs, and the two giggling faces peeping out.

"Did you two just send me a secret message?" he asked.

"Maybe," said Noor. "Did you read it?"

Dad scratched his head. "It said... long, short, short, long. That's a G, I think. Or maybe a cat."

Tansy fell over sideways. Noor laughed so much she had to lie down.

Dad shook his head and turned off the lamp. "Bedtime, you two. The fort can stay up until morning."

So the torch glowed softly. The quilt settled low over the cushions. Outside, the streetlights blinked, one by one, and the house grew still.

Noor pulled the blanket to her chin. Tansy's breathing slowed beside her. Somewhere, the fridge hummed.

Tap. Tap.

Tap.

Then the torch clicked off, and the fort was quiet, and the twins were fast asleep.

The Moonwick Night Train left the station with a long, slow sigh. Its wheels went clickety-clack, clickety-clack, like someone counting sheep in a whisper. Outside, the fields rolled past in soft grey waves, and the stars hung low over the hedges, as if they wanted a closer look at the train.

Fig was the train's lamp-keeper. Every hour she walked up and down the carriages, checking that each little lamp still glowed like warm honey. She wore a woolly green hat, carried a ticket punch shaped like a crescent moon, and tried very hard to hide her yawns.

"Lamps are glowing. Tickets are punched. The engine is humming," she whispered to herself. "Nothing urgent tonight. Nothing urgent at all."

In the fourth carriage, an old man sat by the window with his coat buttoned up to his chin. His name was Hector, and his face was as creased as a letter read many times. He was patting the seat beside him, then patting his coat pockets, then patting the seat again.

"Good evening, Hector," said Fig softly. "Are you comfortable?"

"Almost," said Hector. "I've lost my spectacles. Without them the world goes fuzzy around the edges. My granddaughter gave me a book, and I promised her I would finish it before we reach the sea."

Fig sat down opposite him. The seat smelled of peppermints and rain. The train swayed gently from side to side, and the lamps flickered like sleepy fireflies.

They searched together. Fig peeked under the cushions and found a pencil stub, a button shaped like a star, and a crumb that had been there since the summer. Hector checked his coat and found a folded map, a ticket from last Tuesday, and half a biscuit wrapped in paper. He sighed and offered Fig the biscuit. She took it, but the spectacles were still lost.

Fig knelt down and peered beneath the seat, where dust bunnies had gathered in the corners. Nothing there but a sleepy moth, tapping softly against the little lamp. "Perhaps they fell through a gap," Hector murmured. "Perhaps they're gone for good."

Fig didn't want that to be true. She sat back on her heels and closed her eyes. Clickety-clack went the wheels. Hum went the window. Tap, tap went the moth. Fig listened, and while she listened, she remembered something her grandmother used to say: when you can't find a thing, go back to the last place you were looking.

She opened her eyes. "Hector, what were you doing just before you started patting your pockets?"

Hector thought for a long moment. "I was looking at the moon," he said. "It's been following us across the fields all evening. It's rather a grand moon."

"So you were looking up," said Fig slowly. "Up at the sky."

Hector frowned, then lifted his hand and touched his own forehead. His fingers stopped. Something small and round was resting there, right above his eyebrows, glinting in the golden lamplight.

"Well, I'll be," Hector chuckled. "They've been up here all along, keeping watch on the moon."

Fig giggled so hard that the moth flew off in a huff. Hector laughed too, a warm, wheezy laugh, and he slid the spectacles down onto his nose. Slowly, the world came back into focus: the silver fields, the dark silhouettes of trees, and the moon, round and bright, sailing beside the window as if it had been waiting for them.

"Thank you, Fig," Hector said. "You found what I couldn't."

"You found it yourself," Fig said. "I only asked where you'd been looking."

Hector opened his book and read a page out loud, his voice low and steady. Fig leaned back against the cushions. Her yawn was no longer hidden, and she didn't mind one bit.

At the far end of the train, the engine began to slow. The wheels changed their tune: clickety... clack... clickety... clack. The sleeping village came into view, with its roofs silver with moonlight and one window glowing in a cottage where someone was already in bed.

Fig rose and walked slowly down the carriage. She turned each little lamp down, one by one, until the light was no brighter than a candle left burning in another room. Hector closed his book and tucked it under his arm. He sank into the seat with a contented sigh.

"Shall I wake you at the sea?" Fig asked quietly.

"Wake me only if the sea is very beautiful," Hector said, his eyes already drifting closed behind his spectacles.

Fig smiled and pulled her green hat down over her ears. The train rocked gently, clickety-clack, clickety-clack, slower now, softer now, like a song being hummed under a blanket.

Outside, the stars leaned in close. The fields drifted into shadow. The moon kept its quiet watch over the carriages.

Clickety... clack... clickety... clack...

Goodnight, little train. Goodnight, moon. Goodnight, Hector. Goodnight, Fig.
