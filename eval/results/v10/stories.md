Bob was orange, the colour of marmalade, and he had a white patch on his chest like a tiny bib. Every morning he sat on the windowsill and watched the garden, and every morning Alice sat beside him with her chin in her hands.

One grey afternoon, Alice noticed that Bob was not watching the garden. He was watching the bookcase. Something small and silver was rolling along the top shelf, going tick, tick, tick, like a clock with a cough.

"What is it, Bob?" whispered Alice.

Bob crouched low, tail flicking, and then he leapt. Thump went his paws on the rug. Clatter went the shelf. Down tumbled a tiny silver key, spinning round and round until it landed with a plink right at Alice's feet.

"A key!" said Alice. "But what does it open?"

Bob sniffed the key. Then he trotted to the wooden box under the window, the one with the brass corners, and sat in front of it with great dignity. He blinked at Alice. He blinked at the box. He did not move one whisker.

"Oh, Bob," breathed Alice. "It's a treasure map."

Bob purred so loudly that the teacups rattled. He stepped onto the map and pressed one paw down on a little drawn cross, right beside a squiggly line marked GARDEN.

So they went. Alice wore her wellies, which squelched with every step. Bob walked ahead with his tail held high like a flag. They checked under the rhubarb, behind the water butt, and inside the old wheelbarrow, where a surprised hedgehog blinked at them and then rolled away with a grumpy hiccup. At last they reached the apple tree, and there, tucked between two roots, was a tin with a faded picture of a ship on its lid.

Alice held her breath and lifted the lid. Inside were three ginger biscuits, a pinecone shaped like a crown, and a note in wobbly handwriting that said, For whoever finds this: the best treasure is the one you share.

Alice read it twice. Then she broke one biscuit in half and held out the bigger piece. Bob sniffed it, gave a very polite lick, and then crunched the whole thing in one happy bite.

"Greedy," laughed Alice. She gave him the second biscuit too.

The sun was sinking now, pink and soft, and the garden had gone quiet. Alice tucked the crown pinecone into her pocket, and Bob curled himself around her wellies as they walked slowly back to the house. The kettle was cold. The windowsill was empty. The little silver key lay on the map, shining in the last of the light.

Alice climbed into bed and pulled the blanket up to her chin. Bob hopped up beside her, turned three circles, and settled with a long, warm sigh. Outside, the apple tree rustled its leaves. Inside, the clock went tick, tick, tick, softer and softer, until it was only a whisper.

Goodnight, Alice. Goodnight, Bob.

Zoe pulled her hand back. 'You stay here,' she whispered to the purple point, and she left it glittering in the dark.

Dad squeezed her hand. 'That was a kind choice,' he said quietly.

Then Zoe felt it: a cool breath against her cheek. A breeze. Breezes came from outside. She closed her eyes and turned her face towards it. Pebble, who seemed to have a sense of direction after all, began to plod the same way, one slow step at a time. Zoe and Dad followed him, one hand on the wall, until grey light spilled across the floor and the ivy rustled ahead.

At the entrance, Zoe stopped and looked back at the glowing room. 'I found the most amazing thing,' she said, her voice wobbling. 'But I didn't take it. I left it for the next person to see.'

Dad crouched down beside her and smoothed the wet hair from her forehead. 'That,' he said, 'is the best kind of finding.'

That night, Zoe tucked Pebble into his cardboard box, lined with a soft towel, and switched off her lamp. Her eyes felt heavy. She thought of the purple crystal humming in the dark, and of the rainbows waiting patiently for someone new.

Some wonders are happiest when they stay where they are, growing slowly, so that everyone can find them.

Outside, the rain had stopped. Drip. Plink. Zoe smiled into her pillow.

Goodnight, Pebble.

Goodnight, little crystals.

Shhh.
