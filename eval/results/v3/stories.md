## alice-bob (normal)

**Status:** ok (expected ok) · category: friendship
- note: I chose a girl named Alice as the hero, with Bob the cat as her companion, since the request didn't specify more about the child.
- note: I made the central idea a small, gentle puzzle: a missing piece of moonlight that the two friends help return.

### Baseline (1 prompt): *Alice, Bob, and the Windy Mitten*
mean 4.43, min 4, PASS · 534 words (target 600) · grade 2.9

> Judge: The story has a charming, cozy premise, with Bob's sniffing and Alice's kind trade with the sparrow giving it a satisfying, gentle heart. The most important fix is to give Bob one small moment of his own choosing or mischief so he feels like more than a guide.

Alice had a best friend who was a cat, and his name was Bob. Bob was orange as a marmalade tart, with white socks on his back feet and a tail that curled like a question mark.

One windy Saturday, they walked to the park together. Alice wore her red woolly mittens, the ones Grandma had knitted, with a lumpy pom-pom on each thumb. Bob trotted beside her, his little bell going ting, ting, ting.

At the swings, Alice pulled off one mitten to tie her shoelace. Then a gust of wind went WHOOSH, and the mitten went flying. It tumbled over the grass and disappeared.

"Oh, no," said Alice. She looked under the slide. She looked behind the bench. "Grandma will be sad if I lose it."

Bob sat down in the middle of the path. He lifted his nose, twitched his whiskers, and sniffed the air. Sniff, sniff, snuffle. Then he set off at a trot, with his tail held high like a flag.

"Wait for me!" called Alice. She hurried past the sandpit and past a family of ducks who quacked at them crossly. Bob led her all the way to the big oak tree beside the pond.

There he stopped. He pawed at a tangle of roots. Something red poked out from underneath, flapping gently, like a tired little bird.

Alice knelt down and peeked into the roots. Inside was a tiny nest. Three speckled eggs sat in it, and snuggled right beside them was Alice's red mitten. A brown sparrow popped up from the edge, cheeping in a worried way.

"Oh," whispered Alice. "You've made a cozy bed for your babies."

She stayed very still, so she would not scare anyone. Bob sat still too, with his tail wrapped around his paws.

"I want my mitten back," Alice said softly. "But your eggs need it to stay warm. What should we do?"

Alice thought hard. She looked at the mitten, then at the nest, then at her bare hand. Her fingers tingled in the chilly wind.

Then she had an idea. She reached into her coat pocket and pulled out the crust of toast she had saved for the ducks. She crumbled it on the grass, far away from the roots.

"That's for you, Mrs. Sparrow," she said. "Thank you for keeping my mitten warm."

Mrs. Sparrow hopped down, looked at the crumbs, and looked at Alice. Then she began to peck, peck, peck.

"You can keep the mitten until your babies hatch," Alice told her. "I'll get another one."

Alice walked home with one bare hand. Bob pressed his warm, furry side against her leg the whole way, and his bell went ting, ting, ting, just a little bit slower than before.

When Grandma heard the story, she laughed until her glasses slipped down her nose. "Then we'll knit a new mitten," she said, "with a little brown bird on the thumb."

That night, the wind outside sighed softly. Alice tucked her new mittens under her pillow. Bob curled up at the end of her bed, purring like a sleepy kettle.

Far away, under a big oak tree, a sparrow was dreaming warm dreams.

Goodnight, Alice. Goodnight, Bob.

Bob peered over the edge. His whiskers twitched. "Hmm. That's a very odd moon."

"It's missing a corner," said Alice. "The blanket came off the fence, so the hook must be on the oak. I'll count the fence posts back to the tree. One, two, three..."

Bob followed her steps, tail high, careful to put each paw where hers had been. Eleven fence posts later, the old oak loomed over them, its branches creaking softly like an old rocking chair.

Bob went up first, quick as a spark. Alice climbed behind him, counting each branch under her hands. Four branches, five, six. At the very top, a small silver hook gleamed in the leaves.

"Together," said Bob, and for once he didn't sound certain of anything at all. "On three?"

"On three," said Alice. "One, two, three!"

They lifted the blanket up, up, and hooked it over the moon's edge. It settled with a soft sigh, and the whole sky seemed to brighten, as if someone had wiped a window clean.

The moon glowed bright and white. Silver light spread over the rooftops and the sleeping pond. Alice and Bob climbed down the oak and walked home along the fence, side by side, while the lanterns went out one by one.

The friends strolled over the cool grass, their steps growing slow. Bob purred softly, a sound like a kettle far away. Alice counted the lanterns down to one, and then to none. The pond whispered under its blanket of silver light.

They curled up together in the quiet garden, and the village settled into sleep. Goodnight, moon.
