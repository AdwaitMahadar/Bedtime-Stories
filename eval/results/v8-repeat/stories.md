Alice had a best friend named Bob, and Bob was a cat. He was a fat orange cat with one white sock and a tail that curled like a question mark. Every morning he sat on the windowsill and waited for Alice to come down the stairs.

On Saturday, Alice came down the stairs, and she did not say good morning. She stood in the kitchen with her hands empty and her lip trembling.

"My mitten is gone," she said. "The red one with the bobble on top. Grandma knitted it for me."

Bob hopped down from the windowsill. He sniffed the air with his pink nose. Then he trotted off toward the hallway, his white sock flashing like a little flag.

"Wait for me!" called Alice, and she ran after him in her socks.

Bob sniffed under the coat rack. He sniffed behind the umbrella stand. He sniffed the bottom of the big blue armchair, where a crumb of toast was hiding. Then he stopped, very still, and stared at the laundry basket. A sleepy purr rumbled out of him, which was not what Alice expected at all.

"Oh, Bob!" Alice giggled. "You're a terrible detective, but a wonderful nose."

Alice pulled the mitten free and tugged it onto her hand. It fit perfectly. Then she sat down on the floor beside the basket, and Bob climbed into her lap, heavy and warm, like a bag of flour that had decided to purr.

Outside, the sky turned from gold to pink to a soft, sleepy blue. The kitchen clock ticked, tock, tock, tock. A bird sang one last small song in the garden and then went quiet.

Alice's eyes grew heavy. Bob's purr grew slow and deep. The red mitten rested on her knee, its bobble bouncing softly with each breath.

And the house settled down around them, one room at a time, until everything was still.

Then a sad sound drifted out from the back of the fridge. "Hic... hic... where is my party hat?" sniffed Onion Olive, whose papery skin was shaking. "I made it myself, with a paper cone and a yellow ribbon. Now it's gone!"

Peter scratched his orange head. "We must search the whole fridge, from the top shelf to the crispy drawer!"

So the vegetables tiptoed and wobbled and tumbled. Mrs. Broccoli peeked behind the milk carton. Pip the Pea checked under the leftover pasta. The cucumbers rolled down the sliding drawer, boing, boing, bonk, and bumped right into a pile of soft mushrooms.

Onion Olive gasped. "My hat! It's been squished!"

Onion Olive placed it on his head, and it wobbled, just a little. "Thank you," he said, his voice shining. "Now I can dance."

So they danced. They twirled and spun and shimmied across the cold shelves. The cheese wiggled, the peppers did a jiggly bounce, and the leftover pasta shook its spirals like confetti. The whole fridge glowed with a soft, sleepy light.

Peter tucked his leaves under his chin. "I think the party is ending," he murmured. "Let's rest now."

One by one, the vegetables settled into their cozy rows. Mrs. Broccoli folded her curls like a soft green blanket. Onion Olive set his party hat on the butter dish, where it sparkled in the moonlight from the window. Pip curled up in the tin whistle, and the lettuce leaves drifted down like a sleepy quilt.

So Grandpa fetched his small notebook, and Zoe sat cross-legged on a flat stone, sketching with a stubby pencil. Her drawing had too many sparkles and one wobbly tortoise, but it was the best picture she had ever made. Pebble, who had never been good at sitting still, poked his head out and nibbled a dandelion leaf that Grandpa had tucked in his pocket.

When the drawing was finished, Zoe switched the torch back on. It flickered once, then glowed steady and bright. "Come on, Pebble," she called softly. "Slow and steady, all the way home." Pebble began his careful walk, and Zoe walked beside him, counting out loud. Seven, eight, nine. The echo counted back, a little quieter each time.

Outside, the sky had turned peach and violet. The green hill looked very far away and very friendly.

"Grandpa," Zoe said, tucking the notebook under her arm, "I think the best treasures are the ones we share. And the ones we leave in their own home, so others can find them too."

"That's exactly right, sweetheart," said Grandpa, and he squeezed her hand.

That night, Zoe set Pebble on the rug and propped her drawing on the windowsill, where she could see it from her pillow. Outside, the moon was low and silver. Downstairs, a clock ticked, slow and steady. Pebble yawned, a long, slow, tortoise yawn.

Zoe pulled up her blanket and let her eyes drift closed. Somewhere under the hill, the crystals were still glowing, soft and patient, keeping their secret for the next curious friend.

Goodnight, Pebble. Goodnight, crystals. Goodnight, Zoe.
