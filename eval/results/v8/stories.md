## alice-bob (normal)

**Request:** A story about a girl named Alice and her best friend Bob, who happens to be a cat.

**Status:** ok (expected ok) · language: en

### Writer only (no judge): *Alice and Bob and the Lost Button*
0 issues · mean 4.43 · passed 100% of reviews · 560 words (target 600) · grade 3.8


> Judge: The story's biggest strength is its warm, sensory bedtime close, where the fading sky, crickets and purr settle the reader toward sleep. It would most improve with a few more playful moments in the middle search, so the silliness matches the requested level more fully.

Alice had a best friend named Bob, and Bob was a cat. He was a big orange cat with white socks and a tail that curled like a question mark. Every morning he waited for her at the gate, and every morning he said the same thing. "Mrrp. Let's go."

One sunny afternoon, Alice sat on the back step with her shoes off and her hands in her lap. Something was wrong. Her favorite button, the shiny blue one from her grandpa's old coat, was gone. She had kept it in her pocket all week. Now the pocket was empty and her heart felt like a wet sock.

"Bob, I think it fell out," she said quietly. Bob sniffed the step. He sniffed the grass. Then he sneezed, a small, surprised sneeze, and Alice giggled in spite of herself.

"Thank you for trying," she said. Bob flicked his tail, which meant, in cat, that he was not finished yet.

They searched the flower bed, where a beetle waved at them with one leg. They searched under the swing, where a leaf went round and round in a little whirlwind. They searched the wheelbarrow, which squeaked whenever Alice touched it, so she tiptoed. Bob walked slowly along the fence, his nose low, his ears turning like tiny radar dishes. Alice watched him. He stopped beside the old water tub and crouched down.

"Mrrp," said Bob, very softly.

Alice knelt beside him. In the shadow under the tub, something blue winked at her. It was the button, caught in a crack between two stones, glimmering like a tiny star. She pulled it out carefully and held it up to the light.

"You found it!" she cried. Bob blinked slowly, the way cats do when they are very pleased with themselves. Alice could not decide whether to hug him or to laugh, so she did a little of both.

But when she tried to put the button back in her pocket, she noticed the thread was loose. "Oh no, it needs sewing," she sighed. Bob looked at her, then at the button, then trotted to the back door and sat in front of it. Alice followed him inside, where Grandma was humming over a basket of socks.

"Grandma, can you help? The button wants to come home properly," Alice said. Grandma smiled, found her sewing box, and showed Alice how to make a tiny, careful stitch. Alice pushed the needle through slowly, her tongue poking out the side of her mouth, while Bob curled up on the rug and purred like a little engine.

When the last knot was tied, Alice held the button in her palm. It was warm from her hands. She slipped it into her pocket and felt the solid bump of it there, safe and steady.

Outside, the sun was sinking behind the fence. The sky turned the color of apricot jam, and then of plum, and then of soft grey. The crickets began their slow, sleepy song. Alice climbed into her bed and pulled the blanket up to her chin.

Bob hopped up beside her and made a circle on her feet. "Goodnight, Bob," she whispered. The button rested on the pillow beside her, and the room grew quiet, and the cat's purr grew softer and softer, until it was just a hum, and then nothing at all.

Rocket was a scruffy brown dog with one floppy ear that never stood up straight. He had a nose like a vacuum cleaner and a bark that sounded like a squeaky door. Leo tucked the map into his pocket, grabbed his plastic spyglass, and out they marched across the wet grass.

First stop was the vegetable patch. Leo squinted through the spyglass. "Arr, matey," he said in his best pirate voice. Rocket sniffed a row of carrots, sneezed, and sat down hard. Leo giggled. "Not treasure, boy. Those are just carrots."

Next they crept past the garden shed, where the wind went whoo-oo-oo through the gap in the door. Leo's heart went thump-thump. Rocket pressed his nose against Leo's knee. Then a loose flowerpot rolled off the step with a clatter, and out ran a fat gray cat, looking very surprised with herself. Rocket barked. The cat hissed. Leo laughed so hard he had to hold his stomach.

But the X was still waiting. They tiptoed to the old apple tree, and Rocket began to dig. Scritch, scratch, scritch! Dirt flew over Leo's sneakers. Leo knelt down and helped, using a spoon from the kitchen drawer. Clink! The spoon hit something hard.

Rocket's tail thumped the grass. Leo spotted a crinkled paper bag tucked under the box. It was full of oatmeal cookies, still a little soft. Leo broke one in half and gave the bigger piece to Rocket, who gulped it down in one happy bite.

They sat together under the apple tree while the sky turned pink and orange. Leo leaned back against Rocket's warm, furry side. The marbles clicked softly in his pocket. The crickets began to sing, slow and sleepy.

Leo yawned, a big, wide yawn. Rocket yawned too, and his floppy ear drooped over one eye.

The map could wait until morning. The tin box could go on the windowsill. The stars were coming out, one by one, like tiny lanterns.

Goodnight, garden. Goodnight, pirate. Goodnight, Rocket.

**Request:** A little dragon who is afraid of flying

> Judge: The story's biggest strength is its gentle, repeated sound words like whoosh and flip-flop, which a child can join in on and which carry the flying theme. The title names a character who does not appear, so it should be changed to Pip, and the middle could be developed a little more.

Once, in a cave full of warm, golden moss, lived a little dragon named Pip. Pip had scales the color of plums and a tail that curled like a question mark.

Pip had wings, big and crinkly, like two old umbrellas. But Pip had never once flown. Every time she stood at the cave mouth, her knees went wobbly and her tummy went flip-flop.

"Wings are for flying," said Mama Dragon, yawning a small puff of smoke. "Just jump, little one. The sky will catch you."

"What if it doesn't?" whispered Pip.

One morning, a tiny brown sparrow named Tuck hopped onto the ledge. He tilted his head. "Why are you staring at the sky like it owes you money?"

"I'm scared of flying," said Pip. "My wings are too big, and the wind is too wide."

Tuck hopped closer. "I'm small, so I'm never scared of the wind. Want to try a little hop first? Just a hop, from this rock to that one?"

Pip looked at the rock. It was only a stone's length away. "Okay," she said. "Just a hop."

She flapped her wings. Whoosh, whoosh, went the air. Pip wobbled. Then she jumped, and she landed on the rock with a soft thump.

"Hooray!" chirped Tuck. "Again!"

So they hopped from rock to rock, and each one was a little farther. Pip's wings went whoosh, whoosh, whoosh. Her tummy went flip-flop, flip-flop, then slowly, slowly, it stopped.

But the last rock was far, far away, down by the river. Pip peeked over the edge. Her heart went thump, thump, thump.

"I can't," she sighed. "It's too far."

Tuck fluffed his feathers. "Then let's make the wind help us. Wind likes to lift leaves. Let's watch how they do it."

They watched a yellow leaf drift down from the old oak tree. It twirled left. It twirled right. Then it floated, light as a whisper, all the way to the river.

"Leaves don't push," Pip said slowly. "They just let the wind carry them."

So Pip spread her wings wide. She didn't flap so hard. She leaned into the breeze, the way the leaf had done. Whoooosh, sang the wind. Up she lifted, higher and higher, over the rocks and the reeds.

"I'm flying!" Pip giggled, and she did a silly loop-de-loop, just to see if she could.

Tuck zoomed beside her. "Tweet-a-doodle-doo! You're a sky dragon!"

When the sun sank low, Pip glided home to the cave. She landed softly on her mossy bed.

Mama Dragon smiled sleepily. "How was your flight?"

"Whoosh," said Pip, and her eyes began to close. "Soft and slow."

The river hummed. The moss glowed. The wind tucked itself into the trees.

Pip's wings folded up like tired umbrellas.

Goodnight, little sky dragon.

## fridge-party (controls)

### Writer only (no judge): *The Midnight Crunch Party*

> Judge: The story's biggest strength is its playful, imaginative premise, with vivid vegetable characters and a clever foil-disco idea that makes the midnight party feel real. It could be improved by adding a little more wordplay and silly sound effects throughout to match the very silly control target.

When the kitchen clock struck twelve, the refrigerator door gave a tiny, sleepy sigh. Click. Hummmm. Then a soft giggle floated out into the dark room, like a bubble popping in a glass of lemonade.

Leo crept down the hallway in his socks, his flashlight tucked under one arm. He had come for a glass of water, but he stopped at the kitchen doorway, because the fridge was glowing. It was not the usual cold white light. It was a bouncy, rainbow light, and it was full of music.

"Boop-a-doop, dip-a-dee-dip!" sang a voice from the crisper drawer.

Leo tiptoed closer and peeked inside. There, on the middle shelf, a party was already happening. A round orange carrot wore a paper hat folded from a napkin. Two green beans were doing a wiggly line dance. A lumpy purple potato sat in the corner, tapping a wooden spoon on a yogurt tub like a drum. Tap, tap, bumpa-bump!

"Oh no," squeaked a small cucumber, spotting Leo. "We're found!"

"Shh, shh!" hissed a broccoli floret, puffing up its tiny green tree. "Nobody must know about the Midnight Crunch Party! Grown-ups never believe us anyway."

Leo leaned in. "I won't tell," he whispered. "But the party is going to be a squishy disaster if you keep dancing on the cheese."

That was when the trouble happened. The carrot stepped back, bumped the jar of pickles, and the jar wobbled. Wobble, wobble, wibble-wobble... and then it tipped right toward the edge of the shelf!

Leo's hand shot out. Plop! He caught the pickle jar just in time, and a single pickle slid out and landed on his nose.

Everyone gasped. Then everyone giggled, including Leo, who was very surprised to have a pickle on his face.

"Thank you," said the potato, bowing so low its eyes nearly touched the butter. "But now we have a problem. The party needs a proper decoration, and we have nothing shiny to hang up."

Leo thought hard. He looked at his flashlight. He looked at the foil roll on the counter. Then he had an idea so silly it made his toes wiggle.

He tore off a long, crinkly strip of foil and twisted it into loops. He draped it over the fridge handle like a sparkly garland. He held his flashlight up and shone it through the foil. Shimmer! Sparkle! The whole kitchen flickered with tiny silver stars dancing across the walls.

"Ooooh," breathed the cucumber. "It's a disco ball!"

"A disco foil!" cheered the green beans, and they wiggled even faster.

The party went on, but it grew softer and slower, like a song nearing its last note. The green beans stopped wiggling and leaned against each other. The carrot took off its napkin hat and yawned a big, orange yawn. The broccoli's little tree drooped, and the potato rested its spoon on the yogurt tub.

"Thank you for the sparkles," said the cucumber sleepily. "Now we must go back to bed, too."

Leo nodded. He closed the fridge door, very gently. Click. Hummmm. The rainbow light faded to a soft, sleepy blue, and then to nothing at all.

He carried his glass of water back to bed, step by quiet step. The house was dark and hushed. Somewhere in the kitchen, a single pickle gave a tiny burp.

Leo smiled into his pillow. Tomorrow, he would have to remember to ask about the cheese.

And the fridge hummed its lullaby, soft as a whisper, until morning came.

So Zoe took a breath. She shuffled one foot forward, then the other. Pat. Pat. She listened hard. Scritch. Scratch. The sound was coming from around a bend, and the light there was changing. It shimmered blue and violet, like the sky just after sunset.

When they turned the corner, Zoe forgot to breathe.

The little room was lined with crystals. Some were as tall as her arm, and some were as small as lentils. They glowed from inside, soft and pale, and every time the lantern moved, the whole room sparkled like frost on a window. In the middle sat Pebble, very calm, with his nose almost touching a crystal shaped like a star. It was the biggest, brightest one of all.

"Oh, Pebble," Zoe breathed. "You found it."

Pebble blinked his slow old eyes, as if to say, Of course I did. I was walking carefully.

Zoe crouched down, very still, and looked closely. The star crystal had a faint hum, so quiet it was almost a feeling rather than a sound. Its light pulsed, bright and then gentle, like a sleepy heartbeat.

"It's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen," Zoe whispered. "Can I touch it?"

"Let's look at it together first," said Aunt Marisol. "Some crystals have sharp edges, and this cave belongs to everyone who visits it. We can admire it, and then we can tell the cave keepers about it so they can protect it for other people to enjoy."

Zoe thought about that. She wanted to hold the star so badly her fingers tingled. But she also remembered the slippery floor, and how hurrying had made her wobble. She pressed her hands together and sat down cross-legged beside Pebble instead.

"I can wait," she said, and was surprised to find that she meant it. "Pebble waited for me. Maybe I can wait for the crystal, too."

Aunt Marisol smiled. "That is a very wise thing to say, Zoe. The best treasures often reward the people who slow down enough to notice them."

So they sat together and watched the star glow. Zoe counted the blue flashes and drew pictures of them in her head. Pebble yawned a long, wide yawn.

After a while, the cave keepers came down the path with their own lanterns. They promised to keep the star safe and to add it to the map of Glimmer Cave, with Zoe's name beside it as the one who found it. Zoe felt warm all the way to her toes. She had not grabbed the treasure. She had waited, and the waiting had made the wonder feel even bigger.

On the walk back up the path, Aunt Marisol put a hand on Zoe's shoulder. "You know, you were brave and patient today," she said. "Remember that the best things are worth going slowly for, and that sharing them makes them shine even brighter."

Zoe nodded, tucking that thought away like a precious stone in her pocket.

At home, Pebble settled into his cozy box under the window, and Zoe tucked her blanket up to her chin. Her shoebox of crystals sat on the shelf, quiet and dim in the dark. Outside, the moon hung low and soft over the hill.

Zoe yawned. Pebble yawned back.

The room grew quiet. Drip, drop, went the faraway water, slow and sleepy.

Goodnight, Zoe. Goodnight, Pebble. Goodnight, the sparkling, patient stars.
