Alice had a best friend named Bob, and Bob was a cat. He was a fat orange cat with one white sock on his back left paw, and he could open the kitchen cupboard with his nose when he wanted a biscuit.

One rainy Tuesday, Alice sat by the window with her chin in her hands. The raindrops ran down the glass like tiny silver snakes. "I lost my blue marble," she sighed. "My lucky one. The one that goes glim, glim, glim when the light hits it."

Bob hopped up beside her and sniffed the windowsill. He sniffed the cushion. He sniffed the bottom of the toy box, which made him sneeze twice. "Prrrf! Prrrf!" he said, and Alice giggled, even though she was still sad.

They searched under the bed, behind the bookshelf, and inside Alice's wellington boots. They found a sock, half a crayon, and a very old raisin. They did not find the marble. Alice flopped onto the rug. "Maybe it rolled away forever," she said.

Bob sat very still. His ears swivelled toward the hallway. Tap, tap, tap went something small and hard across the floorboards. Bob crept after it, his tail held high like a question mark. Alice tiptoed behind him.

The sound led them to the bathroom, where the bath mat was rumpled and the cupboard door stood open a crack. Bob pushed the door with his nose. Out rolled a blue marble, bouncing once, twice, then lying still beside the cat's fluffy paw.

"Bob, you found it!" Alice cried, scooping it up. It glimmered in her palm like a little blue moon.

Bob blinked slowly, very pleased with himself, and began to wash his white sock as if it were the most important job in the world.

Alice held the marble to the lamp and watched the light dance inside it. The rain had softened to a gentle patter on the roof. She yawned a big, wide yawn.

"Thank you, Bob," she whispered.

Bob curled into a warm orange circle at the foot of her bed. The marble glowed softly on her pillow. The house grew quiet, and the rain hummed its slow, sleepy song, and both friends drifted off, side by side, into the dark.

The backyard looked different in the dusk. The apple tree stood tall, its branches like twisting arms. The garden hose coiled beside the fence like a sleeping snake. Leo marched past the tomato plants, counting his steps out loud. "One, two, three, four, five!" Rocket trotted beside him, his tail swishing through the tall grass.

At step twenty, Rocket stopped. His ears shot up. He dug his front paws into the dirt, sending little clods flying. Thump, thump, thump! Something clanked underneath.

"Rocket, you found something!" Leo dropped to his knees and scooped the soil away with his hands. His fingers hit something hard and cold. It was a metal box, dented at one corner, with a rusty latch.

But the latch was stuck. Leo pulled. He pushed. He wiggled it side to side. The box would not budge. Rocket whined and pawed at the lid, and Leo felt his cheeks go hot with frustration.

"Hmm," Leo said, sitting back on his heels. He looked at the map again. In the corner, in tiny letters, were words he had missed before. Find the spade, then the sun will show the way.

Leo looked around. Leaning against the shed was a small garden trowel, its handle painted green. He grabbed it and slid the flat end under the latch. With a sharp, satisfied click, the box popped open.

Inside lay a folded paper, a shiny blue marble, and a chocolate coin wrapped in gold foil. Leo gasped. Rocket barked, a loud, happy woof that made a sleepy bird flap out of the apple tree.

Leo unfolded the paper. It was not a treasure map at all. It was a note in his grandmother's loopy handwriting. For my little pirate, it said. Hunt well, and come inside for cocoa when you are done.

Leo laughed so hard he had to hold his stomach. "Rocket, we found the best treasure of all," he said. Rocket licked his chin, and Leo decided that was true.

He tucked the marble in his pocket and the coin in his other pocket. Then he and Rocket walked slowly back toward the glowing kitchen window. The crickets had started their soft, steady song. The tree leaves rustled like whispers.

Inside, a mug of cocoa waited on the table, steaming gently. Leo climbed into his chair and wrapped both hands around the warm cup. Rocket curled up under the table with a long, contented sigh.

Outside, the moon rose slowly over the apple tree. Leo's eyelids grew heavy. The map lay folded beside his cup, and the blue marble glowed softly in the lamplight. Rocket's breathing slowed. Goodnight, backyard. Goodnight, pirate. Goodnight.
