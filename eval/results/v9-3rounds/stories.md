## alice-bob (normal)

**Request:** A story about a girl named Alice and her best friend Bob, who happens to be a cat.

**Status:** ok (expected ok) · language: en

### Writer only (no judge): *Alice, Bob and the Moon Button*
0 issues · mean 4.36 · passed 100% of reviews · 540 words (target 600) · grade 3.7


> Judge: The story's biggest strength is its gentle, sensory bedtime close, with the purr fading like a kettle cooling down. It would be improved most by giving Alice a slightly more active choice in the turning point, so the discovery of the hidden button feels more earned.

Alice had lost a button, and it was not just any button. It was round and blue, shiny as a puddle under the streetlight, and it had popped off her winter coat that morning. She searched the hallway, the kitchen and the bottom of the laundry basket. Nothing.

On the windowsill sat Bob, her ginger cat with one white paw, as if he had dipped it in milk and forgotten the rest. His tail swished slow as a clock.

"Where would a button go?" Alice asked him. Bob yawned, showing his pink tongue, and jumped down with a soft thump.

They searched under the sofa first. There was a dust bunny, a green crayon and one very old pea, which Alice put back in the bowl at once. Bob pushed his nose into the gap and sneezed. "Achoo!" The crayon rolled away, and Alice giggled so hard she had to sit down on the rug.

Next, the toy box. Bob climbed right in, rummaged with one paw, and came out wearing a woolly hat that was not his. He looked very pleased with himself. "Not a button, Bob. A hat," Alice said, and she plopped it back in.

Alice sat on the bottom stair and thought hard. The button must have fallen somewhere near the front door. Bob trotted past the radiator, where the warm blankets lived in a wicker basket. He sniffed. He circled. He patted the pile with one white paw, then stared at it very still.

"Are you hiding something?" Alice asked.

Bob blinked his slow cat blink, then strolled to the door and sat there as if he had never been near the basket at all.

Alice lifted the top blanket. Something rolled out, bounced once on the floor, and spun to a stop by her toes. It was blue and shiny, shaped exactly like a moon.

"You sneaky cat," Alice said, but she was smiling. Bob began to purr, which might have meant sorry, or might have meant that blanket was the coziest bed in the whole house.

Alice held the button in her palm. It was warm from the blanket. She felt a little cross, because Bob had made her search for ages, and a little happy, because she had found it, and a little sorry for him, because he had clearly loved that blanket very much.

"Next time, tell me," she whispered. Bob gave one tiny meow, which was the nearest thing to a promise a cat could make.

Alice ran to show Mum, who promised they would sew the button on together after supper. Alice tucked it into her pocket, where it clinked against a marble.

Night came softly. The streetlight glowed through the curtains, and the wind had gone quiet. Alice climbed into bed and pulled the duvet up to her chin. Bob hopped up beside her and turned in a circle, once, twice, three times, until he made a warm hollow against her feet.

The fridge hummed. The clock ticked, slow and low. The moon button sat on the bedside table, catching a little light. Bob's purr grew softer and softer, like a kettle cooling down.

"Goodnight, Bob," Alice breathed.

Bob's purr answered, very quietly. Then the room was still.

## treasure-hunt (normal)

### Writer only (no judge): *Captain Leo and the Muddy Treasure*

- issue (safety): "they tiptoed down the back steps into the cool evening air" -> Have Leo ask Mum for permission before they go out, then have them walk down the back steps together while Mum watches from the doorway.

> Judge: The story's biggest strength is its vivid, playful backyard treasure hunt and its warm, sleepy ending. Making Leo ask permission before going out into the garden at night would most improve it, and a slightly stronger problem early on would give the plot more shape.

Leo found the old map tucked inside a biscuit tin under his bed. It was yellow at the edges and smelled of dust and cinnamon. An X was drawn in red crayon beside the apple tree at the bottom of the garden.

"Rocket, we're going treasure hunting," Leo whispered.

Rocket, his scruffy brown dog, thumped his tail so hard that the bedroom lamp wobbled. Leo tucked the map into his pocket, grabbed his spade, and they tiptoed down the back steps into the cool evening air.

The garden was full of hiding places. Tall grass swished against their legs. A watering can clanged against the fence. Rocket sniffed at every bush as if it were the most exciting thing in the world. "Follow the map," Leo said, squinting at the wobbly lines. "Ten giant steps from the shed. Then turn toward the apple tree."

One giant step. Two giant steps. Rocket took three, because his legs were shorter, and he yipped with joy. At step ten, the ground went squelch under Leo's sneakers. Then Rocket began to dig. Dirt flew everywhere, like a brown snowstorm, and a clump landed right on Leo's nose.

"Rocket! Wait, we need to be careful!" Leo laughed, wiping his face. He knelt down and scraped gently with his spade. Clink. Something hard touched the metal. Leo's heart drummed like a rainstorm on a tin roof.

He pulled out a small wooden box, damp and muddy. Rocket sat back on his haunches, ears up, tail sweeping the grass. Leo brushed off the lid and lifted it slowly.

Inside, nestled on a folded scrap of paper, was a shiny gold coin with a crooked smile stamped on it. Beneath it lay a note in careful handwriting: For the finder, a pirate's thanks. Leo gasped. Rocket barked once, very loudly, and then yawned a huge yawn that made Leo giggle.

Leo held the coin up to the porch light. It winked at him. "Captain Rocket," he said solemnly, "we have found our treasure."

The back door creaked open. Mum's voice floated across the garden. "Leo? Rocket? It's time for bed, you two."

Leo tucked the coin safely in his pocket and the wooden box under his arm. Rocket trotted beside him, his paws quiet on the cool grass. The sky above was turning the deep blue of a calm sea. A single star blinked on, then another.

Back in his room, Leo placed the box on his shelf, next to his best pirate hat. He pulled up his blanket. Rocket curled into a warm, fuzzy circle at the foot of the bed.

The lamp glowed soft and low. The map rested on the pillow beside Leo's head. Outside, the apple tree rustled gently in the breeze, and the garden settled into sleep.

Goodnight, Captain Leo. Goodnight, Rocket.

## scared-dragon (normal)

**Status:** ok (expected ok) · language: en

### Writer only (no judge): *Pip and the Wobbly Wings*
0 issues · mean 4.57 · passed 0% of reviews · 357 words (target 600) · grade 1.4


> Judge: The story's biggest strength is its lively sound words and playful practice game that make Pip's fear easy to picture and fun to read aloud. It could be improved by adding a little more length and a repeated refrain that a child can join in on.

High on a rocky hill lived a little dragon named Pip. Pip had green scales, a wiggly tail, and two small wings that looked like wet leaves.

Every morning the big dragons zoomed past the clouds. Whoosh! Swoosh! Whoomph! They looped and dipped and rolled in the sky.

Pip stood at the edge of the hill and watched. Her tummy went flip, flop, flip.

"Today is the day," said Mama Dragon. "Jump, and spread your wings."

Pip jumped. Her wings flapped. Thup, thup, thup. Then she tumbled down into the soft grass with a plop.

"Ouch," said Pip. "Not yet."

The next morning, Pip tried again. She hopped. She flapped. She wobbled and bumped into a bush. Rustle, rustle, scratch.

A field mouse peeked out. "Why are you so wobbly?" it squeaked.

"My wings are scared," said Pip. "And so am I."

The mouse scratched its ear. "Hmm. Maybe your wings need a little practice, like my tiny feet on the tall grass."

So Pip and the mouse practiced together. They played a game called Leaf Catch. Pip flapped low over the grass. The mouse zig-zagged and squeaked, "Eek! Over here!"

Pip giggled so hard that she hiccupped. Hic! Hic! A puff of sparkly smoke floated up, and it smelled like warm cinnamon.

Then Pip noticed something. When she was laughing, her wings were loose and floppy. They felt light, like a dandelion seed.

"Let's try that again," Pip whispered. She thought of the silly mouse and the tickly grass. She hopped. She wiggled. She let her wings go soft.

Whoosh! The wind lifted her up, up, up. Her wings went flap, flap, glide.

"I'm flying!" Pip sang. She looped over the hill and waved at the cows below.

When she landed, Mama Dragon was smiling. Pip's tail swished with happiness.

That night, the moon rose soft and round. Pip curled up in her warm nest of moss. The wind hummed a slow, sleepy song. Hoo-oo. Hoo-oo.

Pip's wings folded close, like a blanket. Her eyes grew heavy.

The mouse yawned in the grass. The stars blinked, one by one.

Good night, little dragon. Shh. Sleep now.

## fridge-party (controls)

**Request:** The vegetables in the fridge throw a secret midnight party

**Status:** ok (expected ok) · language: en

### Writer only (no judge): *Juniper and the Midnight Fridge Party*
0 issues · mean 4.36 · passed 100% of reviews · 536 words (target 600) · grade 3.4


> Judge: The story's biggest strength is its playful, fully imagined fridge party, where each vegetable has a clear personality and the silliness stays kind throughout. It could be improved by adding more wordplay and silly sounds in the middle section so the humour matches the top-level silliness target.

At midnight, when the kitchen clock went tick, tock, tock, the fridge door creaked open just a crack. Cold white light spilled onto the tiles like spilled milk. Inside, something giggled.

Juniper crept downstairs in her striped socks, her teddy tucked under one arm. She peeked around the doorframe. There, on the top shelf, a row of carrots was doing the cha-cha.

"One, two, crunch-a-munch!" sang a sprightly carrot named Pip, hopping onto a butter dish. Beside him, a lumpy potato rolled in a slow, wobbly waltz. A stalk of broccoli wore a paper hat that said PARTY, and it was slightly too big.

"Oh no," whispered Juniper. "The party has started without a host!"

The problem was the cheese. Its wedge sat in the middle of the shelf, pale and sulky, with its crumbs flaking off. "Nobody invited me," it sniffled. "I'm too smelly for dancing."

Juniper tapped her chin. Smelly cheese needed something sweet to cheer it up. But the fridge was full, and the cupboard was far away, and the party was getting louder. Pip began to spin in circles. A pea rolled off the shelf and went pitter-patter across the floor.

"Shh, shh!" hissed a leek from the back. "If Mum wakes up, we'll be pickled!"

Juniper had an idea. She fetched the jar of honey from the counter, and a small bunch of grapes from the bowl. Then she knelt by the fridge and held them out, very politely.

"Excuse me," she said. "I think this party needs a guest of honour. Would you like to dance with me?"

The cheese blinked. Slowly, it sat up. A little crumb fell off like a shy smile. Juniper drizzled a golden drop of honey across its rind, and the cheese gave a happy, squeaky hum.

"Oh! Oh my!" cried Pip. "Now everyone can dance!"

So they did. The carrots cha-cha-ed. The potato waltzed. The broccoli bopped in its too-big hat, and the pea rolled a neat loop around Juniper's toes. Juniper hummed along, tapping her socks on the cold tiles, while the grapes bounced like tiny drums.

But the dancing grew wild, and a tomato went rolling toward the edge of the shelf. Juniper gasped and cupped her hands just in time. The tomato landed with a soft plop. "Thank you," it said, a bit breathless. "That was close."

At last, the music slowed. Pip yawned a big orange yawn. The potato drooped against a jar of pickles. The broccoli's hat slid down over one eye, and the cheese, now warm and cheerful, sighed a long, sleepy sigh.

Juniper gathered the vegetables in a gentle heap. "Time for bed," she whispered. "Lights off. Doors closed. Party over."

One by one, the fridge light dimmed. The carrots curled into a tidy row. The pea tucked itself into the crisper drawer. Somewhere, a sleepy leek murmured, "Same time tomorrow?"

Juniper pressed the fridge door softly shut, and the kitchen went quiet. The clock ticked slowly. Tock. Tock. Tock.

Upstairs, under her warm blanket, Juniper snuggled close to her teddy. Her eyelids grew heavy, and the sounds of the house settled into a hush.

Goodnight, little carrots. Goodnight, sleepy cheese. Goodnight, quiet kitchen, and goodnight, Juniper.

Zoe explained about the stuck crystal. Dad nodded. "Those crystals grow only a tiny bit each year. Some of them have been growing longer than anyone has been alive. If we pulled that blue one out, the crack might split, the walls could shift, and this room would go dark forever."

Zoe's cheeks went hot. She had wanted the biggest, shiniest treasure, and now letting go felt heavy. She looked at Pebble, who had not hurried once since they arrived. He was simply enjoying the glow.

"Okay," she said at last. "We'll leave the blue one where it belongs. I'll keep the pink one, because it fell on its own."

Dad smiled. "That sounds wise," he said, and he gave her a proud squeeze on the shoulder.

Zoe pulled out her notebook and sketched the room: the blue crystal, the purple walls, and Pebble in the corner. Dad held the lantern steady. They stayed quiet and listened to the drips, plink, plunk, until Zoe felt calm all the way down to her toes.

On the way out, Pebble led the way, his shell catching the last of the lantern light. Outside, the sun was low and golden, and the air smelled sweet with grass. Zoe looked back at the dark gap in the hill and smiled. "Some treasures are best left where they are," she told Pebble. "That way, we can visit them again." Pebble blinked, as if he agreed.

Back home, Zoe placed the pink crystal on the windowsill beside her others. Pebble tucked himself into his cozy box of straw. Dad switched off the big light and left only the soft lamp glowing.

The room grew quiet. Outside, the wind rested in the trees. Zoe's eyelids felt heavy, and her yawn was long and slow.

The pink crystal caught a sliver of moonlight and held it, very still.

"Goodnight, blue crystal," Zoe whispered. "Goodnight, purple lamps. Goodnight, Pebble."

Pebble sighed, and the room was still, and Zoe drifted softly into sleep.
