## penguin-snow (normal)

**Request:** A penguin who has never seen snow falling

**Status:** ok (expected ok) · language: en

### Writer only (no judge): *Pip and the Falling Stars*
0 issues · mean 4.50 · passed 0% of reviews · 409 words (target 600) · grade 0.9


> Judge: The story's biggest strength is its refrain of flick and flake, which a child can join in on and which carries the snowfall scenes. It would be improved by a little more length and a few more playful moments in the middle to match the target size and the silliness control.

Pip was a penguin who lived on a bright blue shore. Pip had never seen snow.

One morning, the sky turned soft and gray. Pip looked up. Something white was drifting down. Flick. Flake. Flick.

"What is that?" squawked Pip. "Is the sky crumbling?"

Pip's friend Olu, a wise old seal with a whiskery grin, laughed a deep, rumbly laugh. "That's snow, little one. It falls from the clouds, soft as feathers."

Pip flapped her flippers. "Can I catch some?"

"You can try," said Olu.

Pip waddled up the hill. Waddle, waddle, slide. Waddle, waddle, slide. Her belly went bump on the cold white ground.

She opened her beak wide. Flick! A flake landed on her tongue. Then it was gone. It tasted like nothing at all.

"Oops," said Pip. "It melted before I could hold it."

She tried again. She held out one flipper, very still. Flick. A flake landed on the tip of it. Pip stared. Her flipper was cold and tingly. She looked at the flake, and it did not melt right away. It sat there, a tiny star with six points.

"Olu! Come look! It has corners!"

Olu slid up beside her, slow and careful. He leaned close. "Six little points," he whispered. "Every flake is different."

Pip gasped. She turned to the next flake, and the next. Flick. Flick. Each one had its own shape. Some looked like feathers. Some looked like tiny snowy trees.

"Snow is so busy," said Pip. "But it is so quiet, too."

Then the flakes began to pile up. The hill grew soft and round. Pip sank in up to her knees. Crunch. Squeak. Crunch.

"Let's make something!" said Olu.

They packed the snow into a big round ball. It was lumpy and wobbly. Pip pressed two pebbles in for eyes. Olu gave it a crooked stick for a nose.

"Hello, Snowy Pip," said Pip, and she giggled so hard she hiccupped. Hic! Her hiccup echoed across the hill.

The sky grew dim and blue. The snow kept falling, soft and slow. Flick. Flake. Flick.

Pip yawned a long, wide yawn. Her flippers felt heavy and warm.

"Time to go home, little one," said Olu.

Pip waddled down the hill, one slow step at a time. Behind her, the snow settled over the footprints, as gentle as a blanket.

She tucked her beak under her wing. The snow kept falling. Flick. Flake. Hush.

Good night, Pip. Good night, snow.

Pip flapped her flippers. "Can I catch some?"

"Oops," said Pip. "It melted before I could hold it."

The sky grew dim and blue. The snow kept falling, soft and slow. Flick. Flake. Flick. Far below, the sea murmured shh, shh, as if it were telling the snow a secret.

Pip yawned a long, wide yawn. Her flippers felt heavy and warm. Her eyes went blink, blink, blink, slow as a sleepy clock.

"Time to go home, little one," said Olu.

Pip waddled down the hill, one slow step at a time. She looked back at Snowy Pip, standing tall in the white. "Good night, Snowy Pip," she whispered.

Behind her, the snow settled over the footprints, as gentle as a blanket.

She tucked her beak under her wing. The snow kept falling. Flick. Flake. Hush.

## blanket-fort (normal)

**Request:** Twins who build a blanket fort in the living room

**Status:** ok (expected ok) · language: en

### Writer only (no judge): *Noor and Wenceslas and the Wobbly Fort*
0.5 issues · mean 4.43 · passed 50% of reviews · 539 words (target 600) · grade 2.3


> Judge: The story's biggest strength is its playful, specific sneeze-and-sagging-roof humour, which gives a light, fun tone throughout. It could be improved by giving the sleeping bag a mention earlier, since it appears at the end without being introduced.

On a rainy Saturday, Noor and her twin brother, Wenceslas, dragged every blanket in the house into the living room. The sofa cushions thumped onto the rug. The curtains swished like tired ghosts. Noor had a plan, and every good plan needed a secret code. She tapped on the arm of the armchair: three quick taps, a pause, then two slow ones. "That means FORT," she whispered. "Only twins know it."

Wenceslas tapped back, grinning so wide his freckles squished together. Then his nose wiggled. His eyes squeezed shut. "Ah... ah... KER-CHOOOO!"

The blanket roof sighed, wobbled, and slid sideways, flopping onto Noor's head like a wet pancake.

"Wenceslas!" she squeaked from under the wool.

"Sorry," he said. "The dust tickles."

They tried again. They pulled two dining chairs together and draped the biggest blanket over the top. It sagged in the middle like a tired camel. Wenceslas poked it. Boing. It sagged even lower.

"We need a pole," Noor said, tapping her chin. "Something tall and strong to hold the roof up."

Wenceslas looked around. He found the mop. Noor shook her head. He found the umbrella stand. Noor shook her head again. Then he found the broom, and Noor's eyes lit up.

"The broom!" they said together, then giggled, because they always said things together.

They rested the broom across the chair backs, like the pole of a tent. The blanket rose up, proud and tall. But the corners kept flapping free, like pirate flags in a breeze.

"Hold on," said Noor. "We need heavy things to keep the corners down."

She fetched the atlas, the dictionary and Grandma's cookbook. The cookbook landed with a sound like a sleeping hippo. Thump. Thump. Thump. Each corner stayed put.

Wenceslas took a deep breath, and his nose twitched. Everyone waited. "Ah... ah..." He pinched his nose hard. "...ahh... no. Not this time!" The roof trembled, then stood firm as a castle wall.

Noor pulled the torch from the drawer. She switched off the big light. Click. A warm yellow circle danced across the blanket walls. Their shadow hands became rabbits, then a dragon with a wobbly tail.

"Crawl in," she whispered. "Code's FORT."

Inside, everything was soft and hushed. The blankets glowed red and gold. Wenceslas whispered, "Is this our castle?"

"It's our castle," said Noor. "And it's a secret."

Mum's head popped through the gap. "Is that a castle or a laundry explosion?" she asked, smiling. She held two mugs of warm milk. "The roof looks very strong," she said. She set the mugs on the rug, kissed the tops of their heads, and tiptoed away.

The milk was warm and sweet. They sipped and giggled until their giggles turned into yawns.

"Do you think the fort will still be here tomorrow?" Wenceslas asked.

Noor snuggled deeper into her sleeping bag. "Forts don't go anywhere. They wait for us."

The torch clicked off. The room went soft and dark. Outside, rain tapped gently on the window. Noor's eyes grew heavy. Wenceslas breathed slow and quiet, in and out, in and out.

"Night, fort," whispered Noor.

"Shh," breathed Wenceslas. "Ah... ah..." He pressed his nose into his pillow. "...ahh... no sneeze."

The blanket roof did not move at all.

## robot-vacuum (controls)

> Judge: The story's biggest strength is its gentle, warm arc in which a nervous robot learns that slowing down wins over a wary cat, ending on a soft, sleepy close. To improve it, the silliness could be turned up with more absurd situations and playful wordplay throughout, as the controls asked.

Thaddeus was a robot vacuum with round blue eyes, a whirring belly, and a very loud case of the jitters. Every morning he rolled out of his charging dock in the hallway, went bump, bonk, whirrr across the rugs, and every morning he saw the cat.

The cat was named Nubbin. She was a fat, fluffy, marmalade-colored cat with a tail shaped like a curly question mark. She sat on the windowsill in a warm patch of sunshine, and when Thaddeus came near, she opened one golden eye and said, "Mrrrp?"

"EEEK!" squeaked Thaddeus. He spun in a circle so fast that he bumped into the umbrella stand. Clonk. Clatter. Boing.

Ulla came running down the hall in her socks, which never matched. She was seven, and she had a gap in her front teeth. "Thaddeus, you have to finish the living room," she said. "Mum says the crumbs are having a party."

"I cannot," whispered Thaddeus. "Nubbin might pounce. She has claws. Twenty of them, and they are probably very sharp."

Ulla giggled. "Nubbin's claws are only for scratching the sofa. She's far too lazy to pounce on anything except a sunbeam."

So Thaddeus tried. First he rolled in backwards, very slowly, beeping a tiny song. Nubbin yawned, stretched, and Thaddeus shrieked, "EEEK!" and reversed into the bookcase. Next he wore a tea towel as a cape and tiptoed in, pretending to be a mysterious shadow. Nubbin sneezed. Thaddeus fell over with a clunk and lay upside down, his wheels spinning in the air. "Bloop," he sighed. "Nobody is scared of a shadow with a cape."

Then Ulla rolled a ball of pink yarn across the floor. It bounced, wobbled, and rolled under the sofa, right next to the spot where Nubbin liked to sleep. Nubbin's whiskers twitched. She stood up, looked at the yarn, and then looked at Thaddeus. Thaddeus switched on his fan to clean the crumbs, and the whirr went WHRRRRRR. Nubbin leapt straight behind the curtain and trembled.

Thaddeus stopped. "Ulla," he said slowly, "I think she is scared of me."

"Of course she is," said Ulla. "You're loud and you go bonk into things."

Thaddeus thought about this. He switched off his fan. He rolled forward, one tiny wheel at a time. "Hello, Nubbin," he whispered. "I am a vacuum, not a monster. I only eat crumbs. Crumbs taste terrible, I think."

For a long moment, nothing happened. Then one golden eye peeked out from behind the curtain. Then a fluffy head. Then a whole marmalade cat, stepping carefully across the rug. She sniffed Thaddeus's brush. He held very still. She patted his blue button with one soft paw, and it went click.

"Mrrrp," said Nubbin, and she sat down on the warm rug to watch.

So Thaddeus cleaned in gentle circles around her, humming a soft little hum. Whisk, whisk, went the brush. Nubbin's tail flicked in time. When he reached the sofa, Ulla fished the pink yarn out with a broom handle, and Nubbin batted it once, twice, and then rolled over with a happy purr.

"Thaddeus," said Ulla with a big gap-toothed grin, "you did the whole living room, and you didn't go bonk once."

Thaddeus rolled back to his dock, very slowly, through a patch of moonlight. The house grew quiet. Ulla turned off the lamp. The hallway clock ticked, tock, tock.

Nubbin padded after him and curled up on the blanket beside the dock, a warm orange ball with a tail tucked under her nose. Thaddeus's blue light blinked, softer and softer, like a sleepy firefly.

The crumbs were gone. The rugs were soft. The house was still.

Good night, Thaddeus. Good night, Nubbin.

Shhh.
