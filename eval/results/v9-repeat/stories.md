## alice-bob (normal)

**Request:** A story about a girl named Alice and her best friend Bob, who happens to be a cat.

**Status:** ok (expected ok) · language: en

### Writer only (no judge): *Alice and Bob's Missing Jingle*
0.5 issues · mean 4.36 · passed 50% of reviews · 568 words (target 600) · grade 3.6


> Judge: The story's biggest strength is its gentle, sensory world and the warm, believable bond between Alice and Bob. It could be improved by giving the search a slightly more distinctive turn, so the moment the bell is found feels more surprising.

Alice lived at the top of a crooked house on Pebble Lane, and her best friend was a ginger cat called Bob. Bob had one white sock on his back left paw and a silver bell that hung from a blue ribbon around his neck. Every morning he trotted into her room, going jingle, jingle, jingle across the floorboards, then leapt onto her quilt with a soft thump.

"Good morning, Bob," said Alice, rubbing her sleepy eyes. Bob pressed his cold nose against hers, which was their special hello, and they both giggled.

One Saturday, Alice woke up and listened. No jingle. She listened again. Only the clock going tick, tock, and a sparrow chirping on the windowsill.

"Bob?" she called. He padded in with his tail held high, but he made no sound at all. The blue ribbon was still there, dangling loose and empty.

"Oh no," said Alice. "Where did your bell go?"

They searched together. Alice looked under the sofa, behind the teapot and inside her wellington boots. Bob searched too, though he mostly sniffed the bread bin and tried to squeeze into the laundry basket. Alice felt a wobble in her tummy. Bob's bell was how she always knew he was near.

They checked the kitchen, the bathroom and the airing cupboard. Alice found a sock, two buttons and a very surprised spider, but no bell. At last she pulled on her yellow raincoat and carried Bob out into the garden. The wet grass sparkled. The apple tree dripped.

"Bob, we have to be super quiet," Alice whispered. "Then we might hear it."

They sat on the old wooden bench. Bob's ears swivelled like tiny radar dishes. A snail slid past. A wood pigeon cooed. Somewhere a tap went plip, plop. Alice held her breath and listened harder.

Then a breeze stirred the lavender, and from somewhere under the bench came a faint, tiny ting.

Bob's whiskers twitched. He wriggled off the seat and flattened himself on the path, nose pressed to a gap between the boards. "Bob, you silly thing," Alice giggled, "is it down there?"

She lay on her tummy, cold and damp, and reached under the bench. The ribbon had slipped loose, and the bell had dropped through a crack. Her fingers brushed something smooth, then something cold and round. She pulled it out. Her silver bell, a little muddy, glinted in her palm.

She gave it a gentle shake. Jingle! Bob leapt up so fast that he bumped her chin, and then he purred like a little motorboat.

Together they sat on the path while Alice tied the ribbon in a double knot. Bob sat so patiently that she almost thought he was a statue. His ears were flat with embarrassment, but he forgave her with one slow blink. Then he butted his head against her wellies, and they walked back inside, jingling all the way.

That evening the rain tapped softly on the window. Alice tucked herself into bed, and Bob curled up on her feet like a warm, furry loaf.

Jingle, went the bell, as he turned one slow circle. Ting. Then the bell went still.

"Goodnight, Bob," Alice whispered. His purr rolled on, low and slow, like a sleepy train far away.

Alice's eyes grew heavy. The rain whispered, the clock said tock, and the house settled in around them. The little bell lay quiet at last.

Rocket, his scruffy brown dog, was already awake, thumping his tail against the bed. "Today," Leo whispered, "we find the treasure of Captain Bristlebeard."

Out in the backyard, the grass was wet and silver with dew. The old apple tree creaked in the breeze, and a blue jay scolded them from the fence. Leo unrolled the map on the back step. The first clue read: Where the shadow of the shed is longest, dig with a spoon and a prayer.

Rocket sniffed the shed wall, then trotted to the corner where the shadow stretched across the flowerbed. He began to dig, paws flying, dirt spraying like confetti. "Rocket, wait!" Leo laughed, grabbing the trowel. They dug together, scoop by scoop, until the trowel clinked against something hard. It was a tin box with a rusty latch. The second clue was trickier. Climb no ladder, cross no fence, find the place where the wind plays hide and seek. Leo frowned at the map. Rocket sat down, tilted his head, and gave a small, confused whine. Then a gust blew through the garden and rattled the wind chimes by the back door. Leo's eyes lit up. "The chimes! Rocket, the wind is hiding right there!"

Behind the chimes, taped under the porch rail, was a second envelope. Rocket barked so loudly that a sleepy robin flapped out of the hedge. Leo tore open the envelope and read the last clue aloud in his best pirate voice. "Beneath the old wheelbarrow, where the caterpillars nap, lies the greatest treasure of all!"

They raced to the wheelbarrow, tipped it gently on its side, and found a bundle wrapped in a faded blue towel. Inside was a small wooden chest painted with stars. Leo opened it slowly. There were no jewels, only a stack of homemade pirate stickers, a jar of golden honey, and a note in careful handwriting: For the captain and his crew, who found the way together.

Leo grinned and gave Rocket a big hug. Rocket licked his nose, then wagged so hard his whole back end wiggled. "Best treasure ever," Leo said, and Rocket yawned, as if he agreed.

The sun climbed higher and then began to sink. Back in the house, Leo tucked the map beside his bed and the wooden chest on the windowsill. Rocket curled into his blanket at the foot of the bed, one ear twitching. The wind chimes sang softly outside, and the apple tree sighed its leafy sigh.

Leo's eyes grew heavy. The stars came out one by one above the garden, quiet and bright. Somewhere below, a cricket hummed, and the moon slid gently across the sky. Rocket's breathing slowed. Leo pulled up his covers and closed his eyes.

Goodnight, little captain. Goodnight, good dog. The treasure is safe, and so are you.

At midnight, when the kitchen clock gave its tired little tick, the fridge door creaked open just a crack. A cold, silvery light spilled across the tiles, and somewhere inside, a tiny trumpet went *toot-a-doo-doo!*

Hector the hedgehog-shaped salt pot woke first. Well, he was not really a hedgehog, but the kitchen mice liked to say so. He peeked out from behind the bread bin with one bleary eye. "Is it morning?" he whispered.

"No, it is PARTY time," squeaked Orla, the mouse with the very long whiskers. She wiggled her nose. "The vegetables are having a secret midnight party, and we are invited!"

But there was a problem. Ferdinand the carrot had been left out of the list. He sat alone on the bottom shelf, his orange cheeks drooping. "Nobody wants a crunchy old carrot," he sighed, and his leaves gave a sad little rustle.

"Nonsense!" boomed a voice from the cheese drawer. It was Bodhi the broccoli, who was very tall and very green and very fond of wobbling when he laughed. "Every party needs a carrot. Who else will do the crunch-crunch dance?"

So the four friends set to work. Orla tiptoed up the shelf, her whiskers twitching, and tugged a sparkly ribbon from a cracker box. Hector rolled a bottle cap across the floor, clink, clink, clink, to make a drum. Bodhi puffed out his florets and went *ffff-ploof!* like a very polite sneeze.

But when they reached the top shelf, they found the party was already going on without them. Peas were bouncing on the butter. A row of cucumbers was doing a wobbly conga line. Nobody had saved a place for Ferdinand.

Hector scratched his salty head. "A party needs a special place," he said slowly. "A place just for the guest of honour."

So Orla fetched the ribbon. Hector fetched the bottle cap. Bodhi lowered his tall green self down like a bridge, and Ferdinand scrambled up his leafy stem. Together they built a throne out of the lid of a yogurt pot, decorated with peas and a single shiny grape.

"Ladies and gentlemen," announced Bodhi, "our crunchiest guest, Ferdinand the Carrot!"

The whole fridge cheered. The cucumbers stopped their conga line to clap. Ferdinand's leaves stood up tall and proud, and he did the crunch-crunch dance so loudly that the milk jug giggled and sloshed.

At last the party began to quiet. The music of the bottle-cap drum grew softer. Orla's whiskers drooped. Bodhi folded his florets in a sleepy bundle. Hector yawned a yawn as wide as a biscuit tin.

"Shh," whispered Ferdinand, tucking a leaf under his chin. "Listen."

The fridge hummed its gentle hum. A drip of water fell, plip, into the crisper drawer. Somewhere far away, a clock ticked, tick, tock, tick.

One by one, the friends curled up in the cool, quiet light. The party lanterns dimmed to a glow. The mice slipped back to their hole. The door swung softly closed, click.

And in the dark, a small orange carrot dreamed of crunchy dances, while the fridge hummed its lullaby, hush, hush, goodnight.

Then came a new sound, a low rumble, and a trickle of pebbles tumbling from the ceiling. Dust drifted through the flashlight beam. When it cleared, a heap of rocks blocked the way they had come in. Zoe's heart thumped like a drum. "It's okay," she told herself. "Rocks can be moved. Let's think."

She sat down with Pebble and held very still. Across the room, the breeze was still blowing, cool and steady, against her cheek. "Air comes from somewhere," she whispered. "Pebble, we just have to follow it." She crept along the far wall, past rows of glowing purple crystals, until she found a low, wide tunnel near the floor. It was a squeeze, but the breeze was pouring through it, and at the far end, a thin line of gold light shone through.

Before leaving, Zoe spotted a small pink crystal lying on the floor, sparkling like sugar. She picked it up. It felt warm, as if it had been sitting in the sunshine. She thought about her shelf at home, and how it would look lovely there, next to her other stones. Then she looked back at the great garden of crystals humming quietly in the dark. They had been growing here for a very long time, and they would be just as beautiful tomorrow, and the day after, with nobody holding them. Zoe knelt and set the pink crystal gently beside the pool. "You belong here," she whispered. "Stay and shine for the next visitor."

Pebble climbed into her arms, grumbling a little about being carried, and they wriggled through the tunnel together. On the other side, the hill was bathed in orange light. "Zoe!" a voice called. It was Dad, running up the path with his hair windblown and his face white with worry. Zoe ran to him, dusty and grinning, with Pebble tucked under one arm. He hugged her so tightly that Pebble's head popped out in protest.

"You gave me a fright," Dad said, kneeling down to look at her. "Tell me everything." So Zoe did, from the breeze to the bell-ringing crystals to the rockfall, and Dad listened the whole way through. "You kept your head," he said. "And you kept the crystal where it belonged. That was a kind thing to do."

"I want to tell everyone about the cave," Zoe said, "so they know it's there. But I don't want anyone to take the crystals." Dad smiled and ruffled her hair. "The most wonderful things are best kept safe where they are, Zoe. Then everyone can enjoy them, even the people who come after us."

That night, Pebble snuggled into his cozy box by the window, his shell warm from the day. Zoe lay in bed, listening to the owl calling softly across the fields. Her thoughts drifted back to the violet glow in the cave, the bell-like ring, and the little pink crystal resting by the pool. The moon rose slowly over the hill, and the room grew quiet and silver.

"Goodnight, crystals," Zoe murmured, her eyes growing heavy. "Goodnight, Pebble." Outside, the breeze sighed through the grass, and the hill settled into sleep.
