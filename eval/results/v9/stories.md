The morning was grey and drizzly when Alice found the tiny silver key under the porch step. It was cold and smooth, and it had a blue ribbon tied through its ring. Bob, her best friend, sat beside her with his tail wrapped neatly around his paws. He was a ginger cat with one white sock and a very serious face.

"A key," said Alice, holding it up to the light. "Somebody must have lost it."

Bob sniffed the key. Then he sneezed, a tiny, surprised sneeze, and blinked. "It smells of old biscuits and rain," he said, which was not something a cat could say, but Alice knew Bob was clever and did not mind.

They searched the garden first. They peeked under the rhubarb leaves and behind the watering can. They looked in the shed, where the spiders were busy and the flowerpots stood in tall, wobbly towers. Nothing in the garden had a keyhole that fit.

"Maybe it belongs to a box," said Alice. "A small wooden box with a secret inside."

Bob's whiskers twitched. He padded off down the lane with his tail held high, and Alice hurried after him, her wellies squelching in the puddles. Bob stopped at the old green gate of Mrs Pimm's house. He pushed the gate with his nose. It creaked open just a crack.

Inside, on the windowsill, sat a small wooden box painted with yellow stars. Its lock was empty, waiting for a key.

Alice's heart went thump. She slid the key into the lock and turned it. Click. The lid lifted, and out floated a soft, warm smell of cinnamon.

Inside lay a folded note and a little bag of ginger biscuits. The note said, in wobbly writing: For the friend who finds this. Thank you for keeping me company. Mrs Pimm.

"Mrs Pimm must have forgotten her key," said Alice. "Shall we take the box back to her?"

Bob purred, a low rumbling sound like a far-off train, and rubbed his cheek against her boot.

Mrs Pimm was knitting by the fire when they knocked. She laughed a crinkly laugh and clapped her hands. "My goodness! I searched everywhere for that key. And here you are, bringing it home with a cat for a guide!" She gave them each a biscuit, and Bob had two, because he asked very politely with his eyes.

The rain stopped. A thin stripe of sunshine slid across the floor. Alice and Bob walked slowly home, their pockets warm and their tummies full of cinnamon.

That night, Alice lay in bed with the window open just a little. Far away a owl hooted softly. At the foot of the bed, Bob curled into a round orange loaf and tucked his nose under his tail.

The house grew quiet. The stars came out one by one, like tiny lanterns. Alice closed her eyes, and Bob's purr filled the room, slow and soft, until it was the only sound left in the dark.

Leo rolled out of bed before the sun had finished yawning over the fence. Rocket, his scruffy brown dog, was already waiting at the back door, tail thumping like a drum. Leo tucked a folded map into his pocket. It was an old pirate map, with a wobbly red X drawn beside the apple tree.

"Ready, Rocket?" Leo whispered. Rocket barked once, softly, and pressed his wet nose against the screen door. Together they tiptoed across the dewy grass.

The first clue was pinned under a flowerpot by the shed. Leo lifted the pot. Underneath sat a soggy paper note that said, in big smudgy letters, FIND THE PLACE WHERE THE BEES HUM LOUDEST. Leo squinted at the garden. Bees bumbled around the lavender bushes, buzzing like tiny motorboats.

"Lavender, then!" Leo said. Rocket sniffed the bushes, sneezed twice, and wagged his whole back end.

Behind the lavender, a loose stone wobbled under Leo's sneaker. He knelt down and wiggled it free. A small tin box rattled inside. Rocket pushed his nose into the hole and gave a delighted yip. Leo heaved the box out, and his heart went thump-thump-thump.

Inside the tin was a rolled-up scrap of paper, a shiny green button, and a note in curly handwriting. It said, Go where the tallest shadow falls at noon.

"Noon?" Leo frowned. He checked the sky. It was only morning. "We'll have to wait."

Rocket flopped down in the sunny patch by the fence and rolled onto his back, paws in the air. Leo laughed. "Okay, okay. Pirates know how to be patient. Mostly."

The sun climbed higher. Leo and Rocket chased a butterfly around the clothesline, and Rocket tried, very seriously, to catch his own tail. Leo built a sandcastle by the sandbox and named it Pirate Palace. Rocket knocked it over with one happy wiggle, and they both giggled.

At last the sun stood straight overhead. Leo noticed a long, dark shadow stretching from the old fence post, pointing toward the compost heap. "That's it!" he cried.

He dug with a spoon in the crumbly soil. Rocket dug too, with great enthusiasm and very little skill, flinging dirt over Leo's shoes. Clink! Leo's spoon hit something hard. He brushed the earth away and lifted out a bundle wrapped in a faded blue towel.

Inside lay a chocolate coin, a tiny brass compass, and a note that read: Treasure is best shared. Leo grinned. He broke the coin in half and gave one piece to Rocket, who gobbled it in one gulp and then looked hopefully for more.

Leo sat back on the warm grass. The breeze rustled the apple tree, and the bees hummed softly now, drowsy in the afternoon. Rocket curled up against Leo's side, his fur smelling of sunshine and dirt.

Leo held the little compass in his palm. The needle spun slowly, then settled, pointing toward the house, toward the kitchen light glowing gold through the window.

The bees went quiet. The sky turned soft and lavender-blue. Rocket let out a long, contented sigh.

Leo closed his eyes. The compass ticked softly in his hand. Somewhere a cricket began to sing, and the garden grew still, and the treasure hunt was over for the day.

## scared-dragon (normal)

Pip was a little dragon with small, crinkly wings. Every morning she stood at the edge of the highest cliff and looked down at the sea.

"Jump, Pip!" called her cousin Brindle from the sky. "The wind is waiting!"

Pip peeked over the edge. Her tummy went wobbly. Her wings went flap, flap, flop.

"I can't," whispered Pip. "The ground is too far away."

Brindle swooped down in a loop-de-loop. Whoosh! Her tail made a happy swish. "It's only air, Pip. Air is like a soft blanket."

Pip shook her head. Her scales clinked, clink, clank, like tiny spoons in a jar.

That evening, Pip sat by the warm campfire in the meadow. An old tortoise named Mossbottom lumbered over. His shell was covered in tiny yellow flowers.

"Something troubling you, little one?" he asked slowly.

"I'm scared of flying," said Pip. "My wings don't want to go."

Mossbottom blinked his slow, sleepy eyes. "Hmm. Tell me. What does your flying feel like?"

Pip thought hard. "It feels like falling. Right before the falling starts."

"Ah," said Mossbottom. "Then let's try something small. Do you have a feather?"

Pip found a feather near the fire. It was soft and gray, with one curly edge.

"Drop it from your nose," said Mossbottom.

Pip puffed and let it go. The feather drifted left. It drifted right. It twirled like a tiny leaf in a breeze. Plip. It landed on the grass, very gently.

"Oh!" said Pip. "It didn't fall. It floated!"

The next morning, Pip tried again. She stood on the cliff. She stretched her wings wide. Flap, flap, flop.

"Remember the feather," she whispered to herself. She took one deep, puffy breath.

She jumped. Her wings went whump, whump, whump. Her tummy fizzed like warm lemonade. And then, up she went!

"Wheee!" sang Pip. "I'm floating! I'm flying! Look at me, Brindle!"

Brindle did a somersault in the sky. "You did it, Pip! Loop-de-loop!"

Pip looped and dipped and wiggled her tail. The sea sparkled below like a bowl of silver coins. The wind hummed a soft song in her ears.

At last, Pip glided back down to the meadow. She landed with a tiny bump, bump, bump, right beside the campfire.

Mossbottom was already snoozing, a yawn wrinkling his wise old face.

Pip tucked her wings close. The fire crackled, crackle, crackle, softer and softer. The stars came out one by one, twinkling and blinking.

Pip yawned. Her eyes grew heavy. The grass hushed. The night breathed slow.

Goodnight, little dragon. Goodnight, wings.

## fridge-party (controls)

The refrigerator hummed a sleepy hum, and at exactly midnight, the crisper drawer began to wiggle. Lark crept down the hall in her striped pajamas, one bare foot at a time, and peeked into the kitchen. The light was off, but the fridge door stood open a crack, and a glow spilled out like butter on toast.

"Shh," whispered a voice. "Keep your ears on tight."

Lark pressed her nose to the cold door. Inside, a carrot was tap-dancing on the butter tray. Clickety-clack, clickety-clack! A bunch of green beans was doing the limbo under the milk carton, wobbling low and bending lower. A round purple beet rolled in circles, going whoosh, whoosh, whoosh, and a leek in a tutu twirled past like a spinning ribbon.

"Hello?" Lark said softly.

Everything froze. The broccoli floret on top of the cheese turned slowly around. "Oh no," it squeaked. "A human! The party is ruined!"

"It isn't ruined yet," said a small voice from the egg shelf. It belonged to Amos, a potato with one sprouty eyebrow. "We are missing the music. Our band has lost its drum. Our drum is a yogurt tub, and it rolled behind the pickles."

Lark tiptoed to the shelf and looked. Behind the jars of pickles, something went thunk, thunk, thunk. She reached in, found the yogurt tub, and carefully pulled it out. It was cold and wobbly, and it smelled faintly of strawberries.

"Thank you," said the broccoli, bowing so low its florets brushed the cheese. "But now the drum has no drumsticks."

Lark looked around. Her eyes landed on the wooden spoons in the jar by the sink. She could reach them from the counter, if she stood on the step stool very carefully. She climbed up, wobbled, and grabbed two spoons. Plink! Plonk! She handed them down into the fridge.

The party began again. Thunk-a-thunk went the yogurt drum. Plink-a-plonk went the spoons. The beet rolled faster, whoosh-whoosh-whoosh, and the carrot tap-danced until its green top flopped in a funny swirl. Lark tapped her toes on the cold floor and giggled into her sleeve.

Then the cucumber, who had been very quiet, sighed a long, damp sigh. "Nobody asked me to dance," it said. "I am too long and too slippery."

Lark thought about this. She did not say anything at first. Instead, she slid the bunch of green beans gently toward the cucumber, and the beans wiggled into a loop around it. Together they made a long, bendy snake that swayed to the drumbeat. The cucumber's sigh turned into a happy wobble.

The leek twirled up beside them. "Now we are a whole party," it declared, in its tutu, with a proud little bow.

Suddenly, a yawn rolled through the fridge like a gentle wave. The carrot yawned. The beet yawned. Even Amos yawned, and his sprouty eyebrow drooped.

"Is it getting late?" asked the broccoli.

Lark looked at the clock on the stove. It was ten past twelve, and her eyelids felt heavy as bread dough. "Time to close the door," she whispered.

The music slowed to a soft thrum. The carrot stopped its dancing and lay down on the butter tray. The beet rolled to a gentle stop beside a jar of jam. The green bean snake curled up around the cucumber like a quilt. The leek folded its tutu neatly, and the yogurt drum sat still with its spoons resting on top.

Lark pushed the door shut, and the fridge gave one last hum, soft as a cat's purr. In the dark kitchen, she tiptoed back down the hall, one bare foot at a time, and climbed into bed.

From the kitchen, very faintly, came a single plink. Then all was quiet, and the vegetables slept, and so did Lark.

Zoe found the cave on the last warm afternoon of summer. The wind smelled of cut grass, and bumblebees bumbled among the foxgloves on the hill behind Grandma's cottage. Her tortoise, Pebble, plodded beside her, his brown shell dusty from the climb. "Slow and steady," Zoe said, though her heart was going thump, thump, thump like a drum.

The entrance was a crack in the hillside, just wide enough to squeeze through sideways. Zoe clicked on her torch. A breath of cool air drifted out, smelling of rain and wet stone. Drip. Plip. Plop. "Hello!" she called. The cave called back, "Hello... lo... o..." Zoe giggled. Pebble blinked his slow, sleepy eyes, as if he had heard it all before.

They tiptoed along a passage whose walls sparkled like sugar. Then the tunnel opened into a round room, and Zoe stopped so suddenly that Pebble bonked gently into her ankle. Crystals grew everywhere. There were purple ones as tall as candles, pale pink ones like sugared almonds, and in the very middle stood one enormous clear crystal, glowing softly from deep inside, like a lantern left on for someone coming home.

"Oh," breathed Zoe. "Oh, Pebble. Look at that."

Pebble looked at it for a long, long time. Then he turned around and set off, very slowly, into a little side tunnel.

"Pebble! Wait for me!"

Zoe followed the scrape of his shell. Her torch flickered. It flickered again, then went dim, like a tired firefly. She swallowed hard. "It's all right," she told herself. "I can see you, Pebble. You're just over there."

Pebble had stopped at the bottom of a little slope, where the walls were covered in tiny crystal bells. When Zoe touched one, it went ting! Another answered, ting-a-ling! Soon the whole tunnel was chiming like a spoon tapping a row of teacups. Pebble tucked his head into his shell and gave a squeaky, surprised hiss, and Zoe laughed so hard she had to sit down.

That was when she had her idea. Lying on the floor, half hidden under a ledge, was a sliver of pink crystal no bigger than her thumb. Zoe picked it up and turned it in the light. It would look perfect on her windowsill. Mum would gasp. Her friends at school would say, "Wow, Zoe, where did you get that?" She closed her fingers around it tightly.

Then she noticed the wall beside her. Right at the edge of the ledge, a jagged gap showed where a crystal had been snapped off long ago, and the rock around it looked raw and bare. Zoe thought about the big glowing crystal in the round room, and the little bells, and how long they must have taken to grow, drop by drop, for longer than Grandma had been alive. She looked down at Pebble, who was waiting patiently with his head poking out.

"I think this little piece belongs to the cave," she said quietly. She set it back on the ledge, where it fit almost perfectly, as if it had been waiting to go home. Then she pulled out her notebook and drew the glowing crystal, the purple candles and the pink almonds, with Pebble in the corner looking very serious.

"Come on, Pebble," she said. "Let's go home the way we came."

Her torch gave one last, sad little blink and went out. The dark pressed close, soft and thick as a blanket. Zoe took a deep breath. She remembered that the cool air had come from the crack, so she turned her face toward it and felt the breeze on her cheeks. She counted her steps. She counted the crystal bells as they passed, ting, ting, ting, and each one glowed a little brighter as she walked by. Pebble's shell scraped along behind her, slow and steady.

At last a square of orange light opened up ahead, and they stumbled out into the evening. The sun was sinking behind the hill, and the bees had gone quiet. Grandma was sitting on the wall with a blanket and a flask of hot chocolate, and when she saw Zoe's dusty face, she jumped up so fast her knitting tumbled into the grass.

"Oh, you're safe!" Grandma said, wrapping her in the blanket. "Did you find anything?"

"I found something amazing," Zoe said. "But I didn't take it. It's still down there, and I think it's happiest right where it is."

Grandma smiled as if she had known that all along. "Some of the loveliest treasures are the ones we leave whole," she said, "so that everyone can find them and wonder at them. Sharing is a treasure too."

Back at the cottage, Zoe tucked Pebble into his cosy box lined with straw and gave him a lettuce leaf. He munched, crunch, crunch, and then went still. Zoe showed Grandma her drawing, and they both agreed it was the best picture ever made. She slid the notebook under her pillow.

Slowly the house grew quiet. The hall light clicked off. Somewhere outside, an owl said hoo, softly, and then there was nothing at all. Zoe closed her eyes. In her mind, the big crystal glowed, gentle and pink and purple, keeping its light for her to visit again. Pebble breathed out a small, contented puff. Goodnight, cave. Goodnight, Pebble. Goodnight, Zoe.
